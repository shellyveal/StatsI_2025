\section*{Question 2: Political Science}
\vspace{.25cm}
\noindent 	Researchers are interested in learning the effect of all of those yard signs on voting preferences.\footnote{Donald P. Green, Jonathan	S. Krasno, Alexander Coppock, Benjamin D. Farrer,	Brandon Lenoir, Joshua N. Zingher. 2016. ``The effects of lawn signs on vote outcomes: Results from four randomized field experiments.'' Electoral Studies 41: 143-150. } Working with a campaign in Fairfax County, Virginia, 131 precincts were randomly divided into a treatment and control group. In 30 precincts, signs were posted around the precinct that read, ``For Sale: Terry McAuliffe. Don't Sellout Virgina on November 5.'' \\

Below is the result of a regression with two variables and a constant.  The dependent variable is the proportion of the vote that went to McAuliff's opponent Ken Cuccinelli. The first variable indicates whether a precinct was randomly assigned to have the sign against McAuliffe posted. The second variable indicates
a precinct that was adjacent to a precinct in the treatment group (since people in those precincts might be exposed to the signs).  \\

\vspace{.5cm}

	\centering 
	\textbf{Impact of lawn signs on vote share}\\
	\begin{tabular}{@{\extracolsep{5pt}}lccc} 
		\\[-1.8ex] 
		\hline \\[-1.8ex]
		Precinct assigned lawn signs  (n=30)  & 0.042\\
		& (0.016) \\
		Precinct adjacent to lawn signs (n=76) & 0.042 \\
		&  (0.013) \\
		Constant  & 0.302\\
		& (0.011)
		\\
		\hline \\
	\end{tabular}\\
	\footnotesize{\textit{Notes:} $R^2$=0.094, N=131}
\end{table}

\vspace{.5cm}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item [(a)] Use the results from a linear regression to determine whether having these yard signs in a precinct affects vote share (e.g., conduct a hypothesis test with $\alpha = .05$).\\
	\vspace{.05cm}
		\begin{center}
			\(t_{assigned} = \frac{0.042 - 0}{0.016} = 2.625\) \\
			Yields p-value of 0.00972\\
		\vspace{.05cm}
		\end{center}
	\newpage
	In R:\\
			\lstinputlisting[language=R, firstline=145, lastline=161]{PS04_SVU.R} 
	\vspace{.03cm}		
	With $\alpha = .05$, a p-value of 0.00972 indicates significance, suggesting that vote share \textbf{is} impacted by the postage of signs in voting precincts. In other words, there is a significant difference between the reference category (no signage, not adjacent to signed precincts) and this category (signage posted). \\
	
	\item [(b)]  Use the results to determine whether being
	next to precincts with these yard signs affects vote
	share (e.g., conduct a hypothesis test with $\alpha = .05$).
	\vspace{.05cm}
		\begin{center}
			\(t_{adjacent} = \frac{0.042 - 0}{0.013} = 3.231\) \\
			Yields p-value of 0.00157\\
		\vspace{.05cm}
		\end{center}
	In R:\\
	\lstinputlisting[language=R, firstline=165, lastline=167]{PS04_SVU.R} 
	\vspace{.03cm}		
	With $\alpha = .05$, a p-value of 0.00157 indicates significance, suggesting that vote share \textbf{is} affected by adjacence to precincts with signs posted.\\
	\item [(c)] Interpret the coefficient for the constant term substantively.\\
	
	\vspace{.05cm}
		The constant term coefficient, 0.302, indicates our estimated proportion of the vote for precincts without interaction with the signs posted (not having signs posted, and not being adjacent to precincts with posted signs -- our reference category). This suggests that Cuccinelli is receiving, on average, 30 percent of the vote share in precincts without the influence of the signage.\\
	\vspace{.05cm}
	\item [(d)] Evaluate the model fit for this regression.  What does this	tell us about the importance of yard signs versus other factors that are not modeled?
	
	\vspace{.05cm}
		The $R^2$ value of 0.094 suggests that the model explains only approximately 9 percent of the variation in the data. Indeed, we would assume that there are other factors that influence voting much more than posted signage, such as incumbency status, the state of the economy, demographic factors of the voting population and the candidate, etc.\\
	\vspace{.05cm}
\end{enumerate}
